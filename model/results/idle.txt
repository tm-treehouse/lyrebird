==============================================================================
A.  WHAT A 2**20 TRANSFORM DOES TO A 20 Hz QUESTION
==============================================================================
The tripped run is reproducible, so it can be taken apart. This is
the 176.4 kHz row of the datapath verification table, the one
README.md reports at 125.06 dB against a 143.31 dB neighbour.

Kaiser main lobe half-width           : 12 bins
2**20 bin width at 22.5792 MHz       : 21.53 Hz
so the DC main lobe reaches            : +/-258 Hz
shortest transform that keeps it below 20 Hz: 2**23.69 = 13.55 M samples

The band it is being asked about starts at 20 Hz. At 2**20 the DC
lobe covers 20-100 Hz entirely and most of 100 Hz-2 kHz as well, so
at that record length near-DC energy and low-band energy are not
different measurements.

And the loop always has near-DC energy. Its eight levels are 0.2857
apart, so the mean of an N-sample record sits on a grid of (2/7)/N.
    one unbalanced sample in 2**20 is  -128.3 dBFS of near-DC
    one unbalanced sample in 2**22 is  -140.3 dBFS of near-DC
    one unbalanced sample in 2**23 is  -146.3 dBFS of near-DC
    one unbalanced sample in 2**24 is  -152.4 dBFS of near-DC

The same 2**20 record, measured two ways:

      mean removed      SNDR     20-100     0.1-2k     2k-20k     <20 Hz
        arithmetic    125.06     -130.2       -3.7     -148.4        nan
          windowed    143.40     -172.9       -3.7     -148.4        nan

18.34 dB of the reported figure is the arithmetic mean not being the
mean the transform sees. The Kaiser weights the centre of the record
far above its ends, so a record whose offset drifts across the window
keeps a DC bin after x - x.mean(), and that bin's lobe lands in the
lowest audio bins. Subtracting the windowed mean instead removes it.

The same trajectory, continued, measured over longer windows with
plain mean removal -- the original method, nothing corrected:

    window       bin    DC lobe      SNDR     20-100     <20 Hz
     2**20    21.53       258    125.06     -130.2        nan
     2**21    10.77       129    143.69     -173.9     -187.5
     2**22     5.38        65    143.94     -174.2     -189.7
     2**23     2.69        32    144.11     -173.4     -182.0

The loss is gone by 2**21 and does not come back. It was never in
the loop; it was the transform being asked a question 8 times
finer than its resolution.

What the loop is actually doing, per 2**20 block of the same record,
as a count of unbalanced samples:
  +1.00  -1.00  +1.00  -1.00  +0.00  +0.00  -0.00  +0.00

One sample per million, alternating sign: a near-DC wander of
-128.3 dBFS at roughly 10.8 Hz. That is the smallest non-zero value the
output alphabet can express over that window, it is below the audio
band, and it is 18 dB of the reported in-band figure.

D.  THE 15.7 dB ATTRIBUTED TO ROUND-HALF-UP IS THE SAME ARTIFACT

README.md's 'which way ties go' table reports half-up costing 15.7 dB
at 96 kHz, 124.58 against 140.25, and explains it as a static input
offset turned into idle tones. The offset is real. The 15.7 dB is
not.

Re-running that table's own function today reproduces the shape but
at a different rate -- 44.1 kHz reads 124.73 against 137.55 -- which
is already the giveaway. Half-up leaves the same offset at every
rate, so a fault caused by the offset cannot move from one rate to
another between runs. A lottery over records can.
