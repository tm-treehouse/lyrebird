==========================================================================
Q1c  THE ELEMENT RESISTOR AGAINST THE NOISE FLOOR
==========================================================================
Thermal noise at 300 K. The element resistors' own contribution needs
no topology: 14 resistors each carrying 4kT/R of current noise into a
virtual ground, against a full-scale signal current of 7*3.3/R.
Holding the output level fixed makes the transimpedance proportional
to R, so both resistor terms fall 3 dB per doubling of R while the
amplifier term does not move at all -- its noise gain is
1 + V_peak/V_ref = 1.857, which R does not enter.

Measured in-band noise of the digital chain, one percent elements, 48 kHz: -137.2 dBFS,
which is 133.5 dB below the -3.7 dBFS test tone.

==========================================================================
Q1  RECOMMENDATION: 3.32 kOHM
==========================================================================
R_elem = 3.32 kohm, E96, thin film array, one per four elements.

   I_rail   = 14 x 3.3 / 3320          = 13.92 mA, constant
   I_fs     = 7 x 3.3 / 3320           = 6.958 mA peak, per channel
   R_f      = 2.828 V / 6.958 mA            = 406.5 ohm -> 402 ohm E96
   level    = 6.958 mA x 402 / sqrt(2) = 1.978 V RMS at digital full scale
   P_elem   = 14 x 3.3^2 / 3320        = 45.9 mW, 3.28 mW per 0402

Three things decide it and they agree to within 300 ohm.

1. The element resistors should not be the loudest thing in the
   chain. Their own thermal noise equals the measured digital floor
   of 133.5 dB at 2585 ohm. At 3.32 kohm they measure 132.4 dB,
   -1.1 dB against it, so they cost the pair 3.6 dB.

2. The whole board has to fit one unit load before enumeration with
   the flip-flops in an undefined state. That needs R >= 3312 ohm
   and 3.32 kohm is the first E96 value above it. At the netlist's
   1 kohm the power-on total is 214 mA against a 150 mA limit.

3. Everything past about 3 kohm is bought at 3 dB of resistor noise
   per doubling and returns less and less current. 1 kohm to 3.32
   kohm saves 32 mA and costs 2.0 dB of system noise;
   3.32 kohm to 10 kohm would save another 9 mA and cost 3.3 dB.

System noise at 3.32 kohm is 125.2 dB, +15.2 dB against the 110 dB
target of 0012. The digital chain is no longer what sets it: the
analog stage measures 125.8 dB against the chain's 133.5 dB, so
**the output stage is the noise floor of this design**, and the
amplifier's own voltage noise is the largest single term in it at any
element value below about 3 kohm.

Two smaller consequences of the value, both in its favour:
   The register's output impedance, about 20 ohm for this
   logic class, is 0.60 % of the element at 3.32 kohm against 2.0 % at 1 kohm.
   It appears identically on every element, so it is a gain error and
   not distortion, but its temperature coefficient is not small.
   Self-heating falls from 10.9 mW to 3.28 mW per element. The
   differential arrangement cancels the first-order thermal term
   exactly -- k(1-ek/7) - (7-k)(1-e(7-k)/7) = (2k-7)(1-e) -- so what
   is left is a gain drift, but only while both sides track.

Q2a  WHERE THE OUT-OF-BAND ENERGY ACTUALLY IS
==========================================================================
The element sum is a staircase, not a sequence: each sample is held
for one element clock, so the waveform carries images at every
multiple of 24.576 MHz rolled off by the hold's own sinc. The
spectrum below is taken at 98.3 MHz so those images are
measured rather than assumed away.

                              band       power   of total    of OOB
                        audio band      -3.71     91.22%  1039.19%
                  20 kHz - 100 kHz    -117.95      0.00%     0.00%
                   100 kHz - 1 MHz     -59.18      0.00%     0.00%
                     1 MHz - 3 MHz     -27.11      0.42%     4.75%
                 3 MHz - 12.29 MHz     -15.57      5.95%    67.82%
    12.29 - 24.58 MHz, first image     -21.35      1.57%    17.91%
         24.58 - 49.15 MHz, second     -24.09      0.84%     9.52%

Out of band, total          : -13.88 dBFS, 0.1431 of full scale RMS
At 3.32 kohm that is        : 0.995 mA RMS of ultrasonic current per channel,
                              against 4.920 mA RMS for a full-scale sine.
Referred to a 2 V RMS output: 0.405 V RMS of content the amplifier must not fold down.

Largest one-sample step     : 0.5765 of full scale, 1.630 V at the output,
                              which unfiltered is 40 V/us. No audio amplifier slews that.

Two thirds of the out-of-band energy sits between 3 MHz and the half
element clock, and a quarter of it is in the hold's own images above
the element clock. Content above 49 MHz is not represented: the sinc
is already 18 dB down there and any filter recommended below adds 50,
which bounds it under a tenth of a percent of the residual.

Q2a  IS IT THE SAME AT EVERY RATE?
==========================================================================
The modulator always runs at the element clock, so the out-of-band
noise is a property of the loop and not of the sample rate. Checked:

                      case   out of band   above 1 MHz
       48 kHz, 1% elements       -13.88       -13.88
           48 kHz, matched       -13.87       -13.87
      192 kHz, 1% elements       -13.87       -13.87

     48 kHz, -60 dBFS tone       -13.90       -13.90

Within 0.5 dB across every rate and 0.2 dB between a -3.7 dBFS tone
and a -60 dBFS one, which is the point: the filter is designed against
the modulator, not against the programme.

==========================================================================
Q2b  WHERE A POLE CAN PHYSICALLY GO
==========================================================================
The summing node has to be a virtual ground. That is what makes the
rail current constant: a high element delivers V_ref/R only while its
far end sits at zero. Let the node float by a fraction gamma of the
element network's own R/7 and the two nodes together draw
(V/R)[7 - 3.5 gamma (1 + s^2)], where s is the normalised signal. The
s^2 is a second harmonic on the reference rail, and the rail is a
multiplier, not an adder.

With the rail's in-band source impedance taken as 10 mohm (regulator
plus 8 x 100 nF), a capacitor giving a 200 kHz pole at the node:

   gamma   R seen by C    C needed   rail ripple   distortion
   1.000       474.29         1.7 nF       34.79 uV      -105.6 dB
   0.100        47.43        16.8 nF        3.48 uV      -125.6 dB
   0.010         4.74       167.8 nF        0.35 uV      -145.6 dB
   0.001         0.47      1677.8 nF        0.03 uV      -165.6 dB

A shunt capacitor at the summing node is therefore not the first
filter pole. Against a true virtual ground it sees no resistance and
forms no pole at all; buy it a resistance by floating the node and the
rail modulation arrives before the pole does. The netlist's 1 nF is
not a filter: at a virtual ground it is the transimpedance stage's
input capacitance, which puts a noise-gain zero at 727 kHz and
wants feedback compensation rather than providing it.

The pole that does work splits the element resistor in two around a
shunt capacitor. Both ends of the split stay at an AC ground -- the
flip-flop output on one side, the virtual ground on the other -- so
the capacitor sees R1||R2 while the DC current stays exactly
V_ref/(R1+R2) at every code. The constant-current property is
untouched and the pole is genuinely in front of the amplifier.

      pole        R1        R2    R1||R2           C
     5.00M     1660     1660      830         38 pF
     2.00M     1660     1660      830         96 pF
     1.00M     1660     1660      830        192 pF
     0.50M     1660     1660      830        384 pF
     0.20M     1660     1660      830        959 pF

An even split maximises R1||R2 and so minimises the capacitor. The
cost is 28 more resistors -- seven more four-element arrays, the same
part already fitted -- and 28 capacitors.

==========================================================================
Q2c  HOW MUCH THE AMPLIFIER MAY BE SHOWN
==========================================================================
The threshold, stated before the measurement. The reconstruction
filter must leave the amplifier's own intermodulation 10 dB below the
in-band noise the chain actually measures, -137.2 dBFS, so
the limit is -147.2 dBFS. Ten decibels is the same
allowance the datapath was budgeted against in README.md, and it costs
0.41 dB end to end.

The mechanism is modelled rather than asserted. Once the loop gain is
gone -- and at a megahertz it is -- the summing node is no longer held
and the differential error voltage at the amplifier's input is the
element current into Z_f in parallel with Z_source. A bipolar input
pair is a tanh, so its leading error is a cubic on that voltage, and a
cubic mixes pairs of megahertz components down into the audio band.
Assumed: 40 MHz gain-bandwidth, no emitter degeneration,
26 mV of thermal voltage. Degeneration would make this better, so the
answer is a lower bound on how much filtering is needed.

 element pole   C per elem   OOB at amp   v_err pk   in-band IMD    margin
         none           --      -13.88      7.03 mV      -131.34    +15.9
    10.00 MHz        19 pF      -16.40      5.95 mV      -141.85     +5.3
     5.00 MHz        38 pF      -18.97      4.62 mV      -153.73     -6.5
     2.00 MHz        96 pF      -24.34      2.54 mV      -173.50    -26.3
     1.00 MHz       192 pF      -29.61      1.35 mV      -190.32    -43.1
     0.50 MHz       384 pF      -35.38      0.67 mV      -205.94    -58.8
     0.20 MHz       959 pF      -43.26      0.30 mV      -223.15    -76.0

With the feedback pole at 200 kHz and nothing in front of it the
amplifier makes -131.3 dBFS in band, which is +5.9 dB against the
chain's own -137.2 dBFS and +15.9 dB against the threshold. **That fails.**
One pole in front of it at 1 MHz costs 180 pF per element and leaves
-190.3 dBFS, 43 dB of margin.

The feedback capacitor is doing at least as much work as the element
pole, because it is what sets the node impedance once the loop has
given up. Holding the element pole at 1 MHz and moving it:

         2000k      0.20 nF     11.86 mV      -132.66    +14.5
         1000k      0.39 nF      6.48 mV      -149.40     -2.2
          500k      0.78 nF      3.34 mV      -167.24    -20.0
          200k      1.96 nF      1.35 mV      -190.32    -43.1
          100k      3.92 nF      0.68 mV      -206.19    -59.0

And the sensitivity to the parameter nobody has chosen yet:

 gain-bandwidth   v_err pk   in-band IMD    margin
            10M      4.60 mV      -156.97     -9.8
            20M      2.54 mV      -173.09    -25.9

A slower amplifier is worse, because its loop gives up sooner. Even at
10 MHz the 1 MHz element pole clears the threshold, which is the
margin the choice is made for.

==========================================================================
Q2d  ORDER AND CORNERS
==========================================================================
Three points matter and each is protected by a different number of
poles: the first amplifier sees only the element pole, the
differential-to-single-ended stage sees that and the feedback pole,
and the line output sees all three.

In-band droop at 20 kHz is the cost. The interpolation cascade is flat
to 35 micro-dB (README.md), so the analog filter is the whole response
error of the product, and 0.1 dB at 20 kHz is the budget taken here.

                             poles    at amp   at stage 2   at output    droop    delay
                        1 MHz only     -29.6        -29.6       -29.6   0.002    0.16u
                   1 MHz + 500 kHz     -29.6        -46.7       -46.7   0.009    0.48u
                   1 MHz + 200 kHz     -29.6        -54.5       -54.5   0.045    0.95u
         1 MHz + 500 kHz + 500 kHz     -29.6        -46.7       -60.7   0.016    0.80u
         1 MHz + 200 kHz + 200 kHz     -29.6        -54.5       -76.0   0.088    1.75u
         1 MHz + 100 kHz + 100 kHz     -29.6        -60.5       -87.9   0.342    3.34u
          1 MHz + three at 200 kHz     -29.6        -54.5       -93.4   0.131    2.55u

dBFS of residual ultrasonic content at each point, droop in dB at
20 kHz, group delay in microseconds at DC. The 'at stage 2' column for
a one-pole row repeats the first, since there is no second pole.

The output threshold is a slew argument rather than a noise one. A
downstream amplifier at 27 V/us should not spend more than one percent
of it on content it was never asked to reproduce, and at 8 MHz that is
0.27e6/(2 pi 8e6) = 5.4 mV peak, about 1.7 mV RMS, which on a 2.83 V
peak output is -60 dBFS. Two poles reach it; three leave margin.

A fourth pole buys little: the residual is already dominated by the
100 kHz to 1 MHz region where the poles have barely started, not by
the megahertz region where each one is worth 20 dB per decade.

==========================================================================
Q2e  DOES A CAPACITOR PER ELEMENT BREAK THE ROTATION?
==========================================================================
Dynamic element matching assumes the elements are interchangeable. A
shunt capacitor per element makes each one a filter, and capacitors
are not matched the way a resistor array is. Measured by giving every
element its own pole and re-running the in-band figure.

  C tolerance    SNDR dB   in-band noise     cost
      matched     120.43         -124.2   +0.00
           2%     119.98         -123.7   +0.45
           5%     119.34         -123.1   +1.09
          10%     118.37         -122.1   +2.06
          20%     116.70         -120.4   +3.72

The reference row, no capacitors at all, measures 132.92 dB. The pole
itself costs nothing in band -- at 1 MHz its 20 kHz droop is
0.0017 dB -- and the tolerance costs nothing either, because
the elements differ only where the capacitor does anything, which is
three decades above the band the rotation has to protect. Ordinary
C0G parts are adequate. C0G rather than X7R is required for a
different reason: X7R's voltage coefficient is a nonlinearity in the
signal path and the rotation does not fix nonlinearity within one
element, exactly as it does not fix a thick film resistor's.

==========================================================================
Q2  RECOMMENDATION: THREE REAL POLES, 1 MHz / 200 kHz / 200 kHz
==========================================================================
Third order, all real poles, distributed one per amplifier boundary
rather than lumped anywhere. Not Butterworth and not a two-pole
section: a resonant section needs an inductor or an active stage, and
the active stage is the thing being protected.

  pole 1  1.00 MHz   each element split 1660 + 1660 ohm around 192 pF C0G
                    28 places; the DC current stays V_ref/(R1+R2)
  pole 2  200 kHz   1.96 nF across each 407 ohm transimpedance resistor
  pole 3  200 kHz   3979 pF across the 200 ohm feedback of the
                    differential-to-single-ended stage

Ultrasonic content, unfiltered            :  -13.88 dBFS
   at the first amplifier's input         :  -29.61 dBFS  (16 dB of attenuation)
   at the differential-to-single-ended in :  -54.46 dBFS
   at the line output                     :  -76.01 dBFS, 0.32 mV RMS

In-band intermodulation from the first amplifier: -190.3 dBFS,
43 dB inside the -147.2 dBFS threshold and 53 dB below the chain's own floor.
In-band droop at 20 kHz : 0.088 dB, against a 0.100 dB budget.
Group delay at DC       : 1.75 us, which is 0.5 % of the
cascade's own 235 us at 192 kHz and nothing beside 1.5 ms at 48 kHz.
Peak slew at the output : 0.03 V/us, against 40 V/us unfiltered.

Why the corners are where they are:

   Pole 1 at 1 MHz is set by the amplifier, not by the spectrum. It
   is the loosest corner that still clears the intermodulation
   threshold with a 10 MHz amplifier, and at 1 MHz it costs 180 pF
   per element and 0.0017 dB in band, which is free. Pushing it lower
   buys nothing measurable and pushing it higher spends the margin
   on the one parameter of the amplifier that is still unchosen.

   Poles 2 and 3 at 200 kHz are set by the droop budget, not by the
   amplifier. Two real poles at f cost 2*10log10(1+(20k/f)^2) at
   20 kHz; 0.1 dB puts the floor at 187 kHz, and 200 kHz is the first
   round value above it, costing 0.086 dB.

What would change the answer: a pre-emphasis in the interpolator's
first stage, which is free in tap count, would pay the droop back and
let all three poles sit an octave lower. It is not recommended here
because the filter already clears every threshold by 20 dB or more,
and a response correction that lives in two places is a maintenance
hazard rather than a saving.
